ORDER

Entitlement to service connection for tinnitus is granted.

FINDING OF FACT

The evidence weighs persuasively in favor of finding that the Veteran's tinnitus had its onset during his military service and has continued since.

CONCLUSION OF LAW

The criteria for service connection for tinnitus have been met. 38 U.S.C. §§ 1131, 5107; 38 C.F.R. §§ 3.102, 3.303, 3.307, 3.309.

REASONS AND BASES FOR FINDING AND CONCLUSION

The Veteran served in the United States Marine Corps from September 1980 to October 1984.

This appeal comes to the Board of Veterans' Appeals (Board) from a Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) March 2022 rating decision of the Agency of Original Jurisdiction (AOJ).

In December 2022, the Veteran submitted a VA Form 10182 Notice of Disagreement electing the Direct Review docket. Therefore, the Board may only consider the evidence before the AOJ at the time of the rating decision on appeal.

Generally, any evidence submitted outside the permitted window cannot be considered by the Board. 38 C.F.R. §§ 20.300, 20.301, 20.801. Should the Veteran have evidence that he would like VA to consider, he may file a supplemental claim. Instructions on how to do so will be included with this decision.

Entitlement to service connection for tinnitus is granted.

The Veteran asserts that his tinnitus is caused by, or otherwise due to, his military service. See December 2022 VA Form 10182. For the reasons below, the Board agrees, and finds that service connection is warranted.

Service connection may be granted for disability resulting from disease or injury incurred in or aggravated by active service. 38 U.S.C. § 1131; 38 C.F.R. § 3.303. The three-element test for service connection requires evidence of: (1) a current disability; (2) in-service incurrence or aggravation of a disease or injury; and (3) a causal relationship between the current disability and the in-service disease or injury. Shedden v. Principi, 381 F.3d 1163, 1166-67 (Fed. Cir. 2004).

Alternatively, service connection for certain chronic diseases, including tinnitus as an organic disease of the nervous system, may be established under 38 C.F.R. § 3.303(b) by evidence of (i) the existence of a chronic disease in service or during an applicable presumption period under 38 C.F.R. § 3.307, (ii) present manifestations of the same chronic disease, and (iii) evidence of continuity of symptomatology. 38 C.F.R. §§ 3.307, 3.309; Walker v. Shinseki, 708 F.3d 1331 (Fed. Cir. 2013).

Tinnitus is "a noise in the ear, such as ringing, buzzing, roaring, or clicking." See Dorland's Illustrated Medical Dictionary 1956 (31st ed. 2007). Because of its inherently subjective nature, a layman such as the Veteran is considered competent to report the observable manifestations of tinnitus. Charles v. Principi, 16 Vet. App. 370 (2002) (holding that tinnitus is subjective and the kind of condition lay testimony is competent to describe); see also Jandreau v. Nicholson, 492 F.3d 1372 (Fed. Cir. 2007). 

When there is an approximate balance of positive and negative evidence regarding any issue material to the determination of a matter, the Veteran will receive the benefit of the doubt. 38 U.S.C. § 5107; 38 C.F.R. § 3.102; Lynch v. McDonough, 21 F.4th 776, 781-82 (Fed. Cir. 2021) (en banc)

The Veteran asserts that he has tinnitus. As the diagnosis of tinnitus is established by subjective complaints, and generally is incapable of objective verification, the Board finds the Veteran competent to report that he currently has tinnitus. The Board finds no reason to question his credibility as he has consistently asserted that he has tinnitus. Additionally, a March 2022 VA examiner found that the Veteran has tinnitus. Therefore, the first element of service connection is met.

The Veteran's military occupational specialty (MOS) was that of rifleman. During his time in the Marines the Veteran earned commendations for his marksmanship. See January 2022 Certificate of Release or Discharge From Active Duty. As such, the Board concedes in-service noise exposure.

In his August 2021 claim, the Veteran stated that his tinnitus began or worsened in approximately April 1983. See August 2021 VA Form 21-526EZ. The Board notes that this would have been during his period of active service.

VA treatment records indicate a history of hearing problems. In October 2020, it was noted that the Veteran had been experiencing ongoing hearing problems for some time, though tinnitus was not specifically distinguished. See August 2021 CAPRI. In December 2020, the Veteran reported that his tinnitus had been going on for more than 20 years, and his medical provider recommended hearing aids for multiple reasons, one of which was tinnitus. See February 2022 CAPRI.

The Veteran was seen for a VA examination in March 2022 to determine the nature and etiology of his tinnitus. At the time, the Veteran reported that his tinnitus began around the time of his service in the 1980s. See March 2022 C&P Exam. The Board notes that this is not inconsistent with his reports going back to October 2020 of him having tinnitus and hearing issues for more than 20 years. Despite this the VA examiner determined that the Veteran's tinnitus is not due to his military service on the basis that his service treatment records (STRs) contain no evidence of complaints of tinnitus. Id.

As an initial matter, the absence of complaints of tinnitus in his STRs is not fatal to the Veteran's claim. See Hensley v. Brown, 5 Vet. App. 155, 159-60 (1993); see also Fountain v. McDonald, 27 Vet. App. 258, 272-75 (2015). The Board finds that the March 2022 VA examiner incorrectly relied upon the absence of a notation of tinnitus in the STRs in finding that the Veteran did not have tinnitus in service.

The Veteran is competent to testify as to the onset of his symptoms, and the Board finds no reason to doubt the Veteran's statements regarding the in-service onset of tinnitus. The evidence shows that he has consistently reported the onset of tinnitus in service, around "April 1983" or more generally in the "1980s."

Finally, the Board notes that the March 2022 VA examiner acknowledged the Veteran's report that "tinnitus began in the 1980's to present" and then went on to state that the Veteran first reported tinnitus in December 2020 with no evidence of it being present prior to that. The examiner appeared to discount the Veteran's statements from his August 2021 VA Form 21-526EZ wherein he alleged he has had tinnitus since his service, the October 2020 VA treatment record finding multiple hearing problems, and the Veteran's own testimony from December 2020 stating that the problem had been going on for a long time. Based on the above, the Board finds that the March 2022 VA examiner's findings to be inadequate for adjudication. Barr v. Nicholson, 21 Vet. App. 303 (2007).

The Board finds that the competent and probative evidence shows that the Veteran's tinnitus began during his active duty service and has continued ever since. Specifically, it began after years of exposure to firearms and related acoustic trauma. The Board finds that the Veteran has provided competent and credible evidence regarding continuity from incurrence of the tinnitus while in-service with symptoms continuing to the present day. Given the inadequacy of the March 2022 VA examiner's opinion, the record does not contain any probative evidence against the claim. Resolving any remaining reasonable doubt in the Veteran's, the Board finds the evidence weighs in favor of the claim. 38 U.S.C. § 5107; 38 C.F.R. § 3.102. Accordingly, service connection for tinnitus is warranted. The Veteran's claim is granted.
